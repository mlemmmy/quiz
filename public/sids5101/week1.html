01

Big Data

The starting point — everything else this week is either about analyzing it or visualizing what came out of it.

1. Definition

Academic (Gartner): "Big data" is high-volume, high-velocity, and high-variety information assets that demand cost-effective, innovative forms of information processing for enhanced insight and decision making.

Plain English: Big data is data that's too large, too fast-moving, or too varied for traditional tools (like a spreadsheet or a single database) to handle — so you need new methods to store it, process it, and pull useful insight out of it.

2. Keywords
Big Data Volume — the amount of data Velocity — the speed data is generated/processed Variety — the different forms/types of data Veracity — the trustworthiness of data Value — the usefulness of the insight extracted Structured dataUnstructured dataSemi-structured data Quantitative data — measurable, closed-ended Qualitative data — descriptive, open-ended Information continuum Petabyte / exabyte / zettabyte Data-driven decision making
3. Core explanation

What is it? A collection of large, complex datasets that traditional processing techniques can't adequately handle.

What does it mean? Modern organizations generate data continuously — transactions, sensors, social media, video — at a scale where normal spreadsheets and single-server databases break down.

Purpose: to capture and process this data so it can be turned into insight rather than just sitting unused.

Why it matters: the volume of data being created is still accelerating — mentions of "Big Data" in research literature alone grew from a handful of papers before 2010 to over a thousand a year by the mid-2010s.

Problem it solves: without big data techniques, organizations either drown in data they can't use or are forced to throw useful data away.

4. How it works
  1. Generation: data is created continuously by transactions, sensors, social media, and machine-to-machine activity.
  2. Capture: the data is collected from these sources.
  3. Storage: it is stored across distributed, scalable systems built for volume.
  4. Processing/analysis: big data tools process it to extract patterns.
  5. Insight: the result feeds into decisions and strategy.

Raw data only becomes useful as it moves along the continuum — and the 5 V's describe the *challenges* that make that movement hard at big-data scale.

6. Example

By some estimates, 40 zettabytes of data were expected to exist by 2020 — a 300-times increase from 2005 — and most companies in the US already hold over 100 terabytes of stored data. Every day, roughly 2.5 quintillion bytes of data are created — enough to fill 10 million Blu-ray discs.

A retailer analyzing millions of point-of-sale transactions in real time to spot buying patterns is a direct, everyday example of putting the 5 V's to work.

7. Applications
  • Banking: fraud detection and risk portfolio recalculation in minutes.
  • Government: public service planning and resource allocation.
  • Education: tracking learner engagement and outcomes at scale.
  • Healthcare: patient outcome analysis and capacity planning.
  • Manufacturing: sensor data for predictive maintenance.
  • Retail: personalized offers generated at the point of sale.
8. Advantages
  • Finds root causes fast: combined with high-powered analytics, big data can find the root cause of failures or defects in real time.
  • Enables real-time personalization: e.g. generating offers at the point of sale based on buying habits.
  • Improves risk management: entire risk portfolios can be recalculated in minutes instead of days.
  • Detects fraud earlier: patterns of fraudulent behavior can be flagged before they cause damage.
9. Limitations
  • Synchronization: pulling together disparate data sources is genuinely hard.
  • Skills shortage: there's an acute shortage of professionals who understand big data analysis.
  • Meaningful insight is not guaranteed: having more data doesn't automatically mean better answers.
  • Ingestion at scale: getting voluminous data into a big data platform is itself an engineering challenge.
  • Security & privacy: more data collected means more sensitive information to protect.

Common confusion: "Big Data" is often used loosely to mean "a lot of data." In this field it specifically refers to data whose volume, velocity, or variety exceeds what traditional processing can handle — size alone isn't the full definition.

11. Mathematical / formula component

Scale is usually expressed in powers of 1000 bytes: kilobyte (10³) → megabyte (10⁶) → gigabyte (10⁹) → terabyte (10¹²) → petabyte (10¹⁵) → exabyte (10¹⁸) → zettabyte (10²¹). No further formal mathematics is needed at this foundational stage — the scale itself is the point.

The Big Data Ecosystem

Builds on Big Data — now: where does it come from, how is it categorized, and what stops it from flowing smoothly?

1. Definition

Academic: The big data ecosystem is the set of sources, categories, storage/processing infrastructure, and barriers involved in capturing, managing, and using big data across its lifecycle.

Plain English: It's the whole surrounding world of big data — where it comes from, what bucket it falls into, what technology stores and moves it, and what gets in the way.

2. Keywords
Doug LaneyEnterprise ("dark") dataPublic data Commercial dataSocial media data Black box data — e.g. flight/vehicle recorder data Search engine dataPower grid dataTransport data Operational Big Data — interactive, real-time systems Analytical Big Data — large-scale, complex analysis NoSQLMongoDBMapReduce BASE — Basic Availability, Soft state, Eventual consistency Barriers (capture, storage, search, share, transfer, analysis, presentation)
3. Core explanation

What is it? Big data doesn't come from one place — it's assembled from enterprise systems, the public, commercial partners, social media, and machine sensors, and each type needs different handling.

What does it mean? The concept of Big Data itself dates to the early 2000s, when analyst Doug Laney first defined it by three characteristics — Volume, Velocity, and Variety — the origin of what later grew into the 5 V's.

Purpose: understanding where data comes from and how it's categorized determines which storage and processing model fits it.

Why it matters: real-time systems (operational) and deep-analysis systems (analytical) have fundamentally different technology needs — mixing them up leads to systems that are either too slow or too shallow.

Problem it solves: without this map, organizations don't know which barriers (capture, storage, search, sharing, transfer, analysis, presentation) they still need to solve for a given data source.

4. How it works
  1. Data originates from enterprise, public, commercial, social media, or sensor sources.
  2. It's categorized — e.g. black box data, social media data, stock exchange data, power grid data, transport data, search engine data.
  3. It's routed to Operational Big Data systems (fast, interactive, e.g. MongoDB) for real-time use, or Analytical Big Data systems (large-scale batch analysis, e.g. MapReduce) for deep insight.
  4. It's stored following BASE properties rather than traditional strict consistency, trading some immediate accuracy for massive scale and availability.
  5. Barriers are addressed at every stage — capturing, storing, searching, sharing, transferring, analyzing, and presenting the data.

6. Example

A bank's live fraud-detection system checking each transaction as it happens is Operational Big Data — it needs speed and availability over deep analysis. A quarterly customer-segmentation report built by crunching a year of transactions in batches is Analytical Big Data — it needs depth over speed.

7. Applications
  • Banking: operational fraud checks + analytical risk modeling.
  • Transport: real-time route data + long-term capacity planning.
  • Social platforms: operational feeds + analytical trend/sentiment analysis.
  • Utilities: power grid monitoring in real time and long-range demand forecasting.
8. Advantages
  • Scalability: BASE-style systems can scale across thousands of low-cost machines rather than a few expensive ones.
  • Continuous availability: systems stay usable even during network failures, tolerating temporary inconsistency.
  • Right tool for the job: separating operational and analytical needs lets each be optimized independently.
9. Limitations
  • The seven barriers: capture, storage capacity, searching, sharing, transfer, analysis, and presentation each remain real bottlenecks.
  • Uncertain landscape: the tools and standards for managing big data are still evolving.
  • Storage & quality trade-offs: more storage doesn't guarantee better data quality.
  • Security & privacy: distributing data across many sources and systems increases exposure.

Common confusion: BASE is sometimes assumed to mean "unreliable." It doesn't — it's a deliberate trade-off (favoring availability and scale over instant consistency) used because traditional strict-consistency databases can't scale to big data's size.

11. Mathematical / formula component

Mathematical component: Not applicable at this foundational level. BASE (Basic Availability, Soft state, Eventual consistency) is a set of design properties, not a formula — the key idea to remember is that "eventual consistency" means all users will see the same value for an item once enough time passes without further updates.

03

Descriptive, Predictive & Prescriptive Analytics

Builds on The Big Data Ecosystem — once data is captured and stored, this is how it's actually turned into decisions.

1. Definition

Academic: Big data analytics is the examination of large datasets to uncover patterns, correlations, and insights, commonly classified into descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive analytics based on the question each answers.

Plain English: Three levels of getting smarter from data — descriptive tells you what happened, predictive tells you what's likely to happen next, and prescriptive tells you what to actually do about it.

2. Keywords
Descriptive analyticsPredictive analyticsPrescriptive analytics Data aggregationData mining Regression analysisMachine learningAlgorithm ForecastingOptimizationRecommendation engine Business AnalyticsBusiness Intelligence (BI) DashboardReturn on Investment (ROI)OLAP
3. Core explanation

What is it? A ladder of analytics maturity — hindsight, insight, foresight, and optimization — each stage answering a harder question than the last.

What does it mean? Descriptive analytics summarizes what already happened; predictive analytics forecasts what's likely to happen; prescriptive analytics recommends what to do about it.

Purpose: to move organizations from simply reporting the past toward actively shaping the future.

Why it matters: value and difficulty both increase as you climb the ladder — descriptive reporting is common in traditional BI, but prescriptive analytics is where the highest-value, hardest-to-build systems live.

Problem it solves: raw data and even accurate forecasts don't act on their own — prescriptive analytics closes the loop from insight to action.

4. How it works
  1. Descriptive: aggregate and mine historical data — gather and sort it, then search it for patterns and meaning.
  2. Predictive: apply statistical models, algorithms, and machine learning to that historical data to forecast future behavior.
  3. Prescriptive: starting from a defined problem, apply algorithmic models to the predicted outcomes to generate a specific recommendation or automated decision.
5. Diagram 
VALUE
  ▲
  │                                   Prescriptive
  │                        Predictive   (optimization)
  │                        (foresight)
  │          Diagnostic
  │          (insight)

Each step up asks "how can we make it happen?" a little more directly than the last — hindsight (what happened) → insight (why) → foresight (what will happen) → optimization (what should we do).

6. Example

A training manager uses predictive analysis to discover that most learners without a particular skill won't complete a newly launched course. A prescriptive system then detects those at-risk learners and automatically sends them a recommendation to complete an additional training resource that fills the missing skill — turning a forecast into an action.

7. Applications
  • Learning & development: LMS dashboards showing completion rates (descriptive), forecasting who's falling behind (predictive), and auto-recommending resources (prescriptive).
  • Healthcare: prescriptive analytics for capital investment planning using demographic and health trend data.
  • Marketing: predictive targeting of likely customers.
  • Finance: descriptive ROI reporting and predictive risk forecasting.
8. Advantages
  • Descriptive: quick, easy ROI reporting; identifies performance gaps and at-risk individuals early; highlights successes worth reinforcing.
  • Predictive: personalizes support by spotting gaps, strengths and weaknesses; helps retain talent by forecasting career/skill needs; enables early intervention before performance suffers.
  • Prescriptive: turns forecasts directly into targeted, automated recommendations rather than leaving action up to guesswork.
9. Limitations
  • Descriptive analytics only looks backward — it can't explain why something happened or what comes next.
  • Predictive accuracy is bounded by data quality — biased or incomplete historical data produces biased forecasts.
  • Prescriptive analytics needs a clearly defined problem — a recommendation can't be generated without first knowing what to look for.
  • Recommendation quality is only as good as the algorithm and data behind it — a weak model produces confidently wrong advice.
10. Comparisons

Common confusion: predictive and prescriptive analytics are often used interchangeably; predictive stops at a forecast, while prescriptive goes further and tells you what action to take about that forecast.

11. Mathematical / formula component

Mathematical component: Not applicable at this foundational level. Predictive analytics does draw on regression analysis and machine learning models, but the specific formulas are beyond Week 1's scope — the key idea for now is simply that these models learn from historical data to forecast future values.

MEMORY ANCHOR"What happened → what's next → what to do." The three analytics types are just those three questions, in order.
MUST REMEMBER

  2. Data aggregation + data mining are the two core techniques behind descriptive analytics.
  3. Predictive analytics relies on algorithms, regression, and machine learning applied to historical data.
  4. Prescriptive analytics must start from a clearly defined problem to generate a useful recommendation.
  5. Value and difficulty both increase moving from descriptive → predictive → prescriptive.

Data Visualization Fundamentals

Builds on Analytics Types — once analysis produces an answer, visualization is how that answer actually gets communicated.

1. Definition

Academic: Data visualization is the act of taking information (data) and placing it into a visual context, such as a chart or map, so patterns, trends, and outliers become easier for the human brain to detect.

Plain English: It's turning numbers into pictures, because people spot patterns in a chart far faster than in a table of raw numbers.

2. Keywords
Data visualization Data discovery — breaking complex data into understandable groups/relationships Visualization tool Declarative purpose — the chart makes a statement Exploratory purpose — the chart looks for new ideas Conceptual data — qualitative, idea-based Data-driven visualization — quantitative, dataset-based Idea illustrationIdea generation Everyday datavizVisual discovery Data scienceDashboard
3. Core explanation

What is it? The visual translation layer between raw analytics output and human understanding.

What does it mean? Data discovery is the process of breaking complex data collections into information users can actually manage — it answers "what does it all mean?"; visualization is its representation.

Purpose: to make big and small data easier for the human brain to understand, and to make it easier to detect patterns, trends, and outliers.

Why it matters: data science is not one single process — data visualization is a subset of it, and often the only part a non-technical audience ever actually sees.

Problem it solves: it turns incomprehensible mounds of raw data into organized, interpretable groups, sets, and relationships.

4. How it works
  1. Data discovery: break down complex data collections into groups, sets, and relationships.
  2. Decide the purpose: is the information conceptual (qualitative) or data-driven (quantitative)? Is the visual meant to be declarative (make a statement) or exploratory (look for new ideas)?
  3. Choose the right tool/chart type for that purpose — not every visualization tool suits every purpose.
  4. Build and present the visualization to its audience.
5. Diagram

The vertical axis asks "make a statement, or go looking?" The horizontal axis asks "is this an idea, or an actual dataset?" — together they define four distinct visualization purposes.

6. Example

An infographic explaining a concept with icons and simple diagrams is conceptual + declarative ("idea illustration"). An interactive sales dashboard that analysts explore to hunt for unexpected patterns is data-driven + exploratory ("visual discovery").

7. Applications
  • Business dashboards: real-time performance tracking.
  • Infographics: communicating a concept quickly to a broad audience.
  • Exploratory data analysis: analysts searching for hidden patterns.
  • Journalism: explaining complex public data (e.g. election maps, economic trends) to general readers.
8. Advantages
  • Instant pattern recognition: outliers and trends that are invisible in a table jump out visually.
  • Condenses large data: a single chart can represent millions of data points in a small space.
  • Faster decisions: stakeholders grasp a well-made chart in seconds.
  • Accessible to non-technical audiences: visuals don't require statistical training to interpret.
9. Limitations
  • Not automatic: a spreadsheet like Excel is a general data tool, not a dedicated visualization tool — the two solve different problems.
  • Can mislead: a poorly designed chart can distort perception just as easily as a good one clarifies it.
  • Wrong type, wrong result: choosing a chart that doesn't match the data's actual purpose (declarative vs exploratory) reduces clarity instead of adding it.

Common confusion: data science and data visualization are sometimes treated as separate fields, but they aren't two different entities — data visualization is a subset of data science, not an alternative to it.

Principles of Good Chart Design

Builds on Data Visualization Fundamentals — now that you're building a chart, these are the rules that keep it honest and clear.

1. Definition

Academic (adapted from Tufte, 2009): The principles of good chart design hold that a chart should tell a story, maintain graphical integrity, and minimize graphical complexity.

Plain English: Rules that stop a chart from being boring, misleading, or cluttered — so the data itself, not the decoration, does the talking.

2. Keywords
Edward Tufte Graphical integrity — the visual accurately reflects the data Lie Factor — size of effect shown ÷ size of effect in data Data-ink ratio — data-ink ÷ total ink used in a graphic Chartjunk — unnecessary visual clutter 3D chart distortionMoiré effect "The Grid" (as chartjunk) Tell a storyErase ink whenever possible Table vs chart
3. Core explanation

What is it? A compact set of design rules distilled from Edward Tufte's work on quantitative information design.

What does it mean? Charts are often used to persuade, so poor design — intentional or not — can mislead an audience about what the data actually shows.

Purpose: to keep a reader's attention on the data itself, not on decoration.

Why it matters: "if the statistics are boring, then you've got the wrong numbers" (Tufte) — a well-designed chart should be interesting because the data is interesting, not because of visual effects layered on top.

Problem it solves: visually busy or numerically distorted charts waste the reader's time, or actively deceive them.

4. How it works
  1. Principle 1 — Tell a story: graphics should be clear on their own, enable meaningful comparison, and yield insight beyond the text.
  2. Principle 2 — Graphical integrity: the visual size of an effect shown must match its real size in the data; measured with Tufte's Lie Factor.
  3. Principle 3 — Minimize graphical complexity: generally, simpler is better — erase "chartjunk" and keep the data-ink ratio high.
5. Diagram 
1  The chart should tell a story
2  The chart should have graphical integrity
3  The chart should minimize graphical complexity

        "Above all else, show the data."

6. Example

A fuel-economy chart in Tufte's own analysis had a Lie Factor of roughly 9.4 — the visual change looked almost 10 times bigger than the real change in the data, a serious graphical-integrity violation. A 3D bar chart can distort bar heights through perspective, and a chart drowning in gridlines, background art, or gradient fills raises chartjunk while lowering the data-ink ratio. Sometimes the best answer is neither: for a few data points, a plain table (like a box score) can carry more information in less space, and more precisely, than a chart.

7. Applications
  • Business reporting: keeping performance charts honest and easy to scan.
  • Journalism: presenting public data (elections, economics) without accidental distortion.
  • Dashboards: avoiding chartjunk so users can act on data quickly.
  • Scientific publishing: maintaining graphical integrity is often a peer-review requirement.
8. Advantages
  • Builds trust: an honest Lie Factor means readers can rely on what they see.
  • Faster comprehension: a high data-ink ratio means less visual noise to filter out.
  • Prevents accidental (or deliberate) misrepresentation.
  • Saves the reader's attention for what actually matters in the data.
9. Limitations
  • Over-minimizing can strip useful context: some labels or gridlines genuinely help interpretation.
  • Not all data is simple: multi-variable comparisons sometimes need more visual complexity than a minimalist chart allows.
  • "Simpler" isn't automatic: a well-designed table can beat a chart entirely for small, precise datasets.
10. Comparisons
ChartTable
Best forSpotting trends/patterns across many pointsSmall numbers of precise, exact values
PrecisionApproximate, visualExact, numeric
ExampleSales trend over a yearA box score with every player's exact stats

Common confusion: "minimize complexity" is sometimes read as "remove everything decorative," but the actual rule is to erase ink that isn't data — necessary labels and structure stay; ink that isn't carrying information goes.

11. Mathematical / formula component

Lie Factor = size of effect shown in graphic ÷ size of effect shown in the data. A value near 1 is honest; well above or below 1 means the chart exaggerates or understates the real change.

Data-ink ratio = data-ink ÷ total ink used in the graphic. A ratio near 1 means almost all the ink on the page is carrying information — the goal Tufte describes as "erase ink whenever possible."

MEMORY ANCHOR"Tell it straight, size it right, cut the clutter." Story, integrity, simplicity — in that order.
MUST REMEMBER
  1. Three principles: tell a story, maintain graphical integrity, minimize graphical complexity.
  2. Lie Factor = size of effect shown ÷ size of effect in data — should be close to 1.
  3. Data-ink ratio = data-ink ÷ total ink used — should be close to 1; excess is chartjunk.
  4. 3D effects and moiré patterns are classic chartjunk that distort or clutter a chart.
  5. For small, precise datasets, a table can carry more information, more precisely, than a chart.

History & Perception of Data Visualization

Builds on Good Chart Design — the rules you just learned came from real people solving real problems, and from research on how eyes actually read a chart.

1. Definition

Academic: The history of data visualization traces the key figures and milestones that shaped how data is visually encoded, alongside the perceptual research explaining how humans actually read visual information.

Plain English: The story of who invented today's charting conventions, and what science says about how our eyes and brains process them.

3. Core explanation

What is it? A lineage of statisticians, nurses, engineers, and designers, each of whom contributed a rule or technique still in use today.

What does it mean? Modern conventions — the box plot, minimalist design, formal visual grammar — aren't arbitrary; they were each built to solve a specific communication problem.

Purpose: to show that visualization is a mature, evidence-based discipline rather than decoration layered on top of data.

Why it matters: perceptual research explains why the design principles from the previous concept work — they're not just taste, they match how human vision actually processes a scene.

Problem it solves: without this grounding, "good visualization" looks like a matter of arbitrary opinion rather than a field with tested, reusable rules.

4. How it works
  1. Minard (1812/1869): mapped Napoleon's Russian campaign, encoding troop numbers, direction, temperature, and geography in a single chart — still called one of the best data graphics ever made.
  2. Nightingale (1858): invented the coxcomb (polar area) chart to show causes of military mortality, pioneering applied statistics and hospital sanitation reform.
  3. Brinton (1914): wrote the first business book on visualization, giving rules for presenting facts graphically.
  4. Spear (1952/1969): invented the box plot and produced practical charting guidance used by US government agencies.
  5. Bertin (1967): defined seven visual variables (position, size, shape, color, brightness, orientation, texture) and the principles of expressiveness ("say everything you have to say, no more, no less") and effectiveness.
  6. Tufte (1983): set disciplined, minimalist design principles — the Lie Factor and data-ink ratio from the previous concept.
  7. Mackinlay (1986): helped automate visual encoding in software, laying groundwork for tools like Tableau.

Alongside this history, perceptual research on "when a chart hits our eyes" shows: visuals aren't read in a predictable linear way; we see what stands out first; we notice only a few visual elements at once; we seek meaning and connections; and we rely on visual conventions and metaphors we already know.

6. Example

A modern dashboard's use of size and color to draw the eye to the most important number directly follows Bertin's visual variables and the perceptual rule "we see what stands out first." A box plot on any modern analytics dashboard descends directly from Spear's 1952 invention — decades before dashboards existed.

7. Applications
  • Modern BI tools: Tableau and Power BI build on Mackinlay's automated visual-encoding research.
  • Journalism infographics: still apply Nightingale-style persuasive, story-driven charts.
  • UX/dashboard design guidelines: draw directly on Bertin's visual variables and perceptual research.
  • Statistical software: box plots (Spear) remain a default chart type in nearly every analytics package.
8. Advantages
  • Evidence-based design: designers can rely on tested conventions instead of guesswork.
  • Explains "why": shows the specific problem each convention (box plot, coxcomb, visual variables) was built to solve.
  • Predicts what will and won't work based on how human perception actually operates.
9. Limitations
  • Old conventions, new medium: rules from the 1800s–1980s don't always map cleanly onto interactive, real-time digital dashboards.
  • Can stifle innovation: over-reliance on "classic" rules may discourage new visual forms suited to big data's scale and velocity.
  • Perception research has limits: individual differences in how people read charts mean no single convention works for every audience.
